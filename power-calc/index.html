Is a home server worth it?

The honest answer is that it depends almost entirely on two numbers: what you pay per kilowatt-hour, and how many watts the machine draws while doing nothing. Everything else is noise.

Idle draw is the whole game

A homelab is idle roughly 95% of the time. A machine that idles at 15 W costs about a fifth of one that idles at 75 W, regardless of how fast either one is when you actually use it. This is why a small modern mini PC often beats a free second-hand enterprise server: the server costs nothing to acquire and hundreds a year to leave switched on.

The rate you pay matters more than the hardware you buy

The same machine can cost three times as much to run in one country as another. Anyone telling you a home server is or isn't worth it without asking your rate is describing their own electricity bill, not yours.

Where self-hosting genuinely wins

  • Storage. Bulk storage is where cloud pricing is least forgiving, and where owning drives pays back quickest.
  • Things that run constantly and cheaply. Home Assistant, a DNS filter, a reverse proxy — a few watts, replacing several subscriptions.
  • Anything where you'd otherwise pay per seat or per gigabyte.

Where it usually doesn't

  • Anything that must not go down. You are the on-call rota.
  • Bursty compute. Paying for idle capacity 24/7 to use it two hours a month is exactly what renting is for.
  • When your electricity is expensive and the hardware is old. A free server at a high rate is not free.

Measure, don't estimate

The device figures in this calculator are reasonable typical values, but your actual draw depends on your drives, your fans, your PSU's efficiency at low load, and what you're running. A plug-in energy meter costs less than a month of being wrong, and turns this calculator from an estimate into an answer.
